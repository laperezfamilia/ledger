# ¡Agrupa! (formerly "The No-Name Game") — Design Principles v0.1

### Purpose

The Design Principles translate the Design Constitution into operational guidance for authors and reviewers.

They answer the question: *Given the Design Constitution, how should an author think when making design decisions?*

These principles describe enduring professional judgment rather than specific authoring methods.

Methods may evolve. Prompts may evolve. Authoring workflows may evolve. Research may replace current assumptions.

The professional judgment expressed by these principles should remain substantially more stable than any individual implementation.

The Design Principles therefore occupy the bridge between the Design Constitution (the project's enduring identity and commitments), the Evidence Base (what current research suggests), and the Research Change Log (what experimentation has demonstrated). In turn, these principles may inform future authoring methods and a future Canonical Prompt as those later layers are developed and validated.

The direction of authority is intentional. **Methods derive from principles. Principles do not derive from methods.**

The purpose of this document is therefore to define the enduring judgment expected of anyone designing puzzles for The No-Name Game, while allowing specific workflows, prompts, and authoring techniques to evolve through continued research.

If a statement would remain true after completely rewriting the authoring prompt several years from now, it likely belongs in this document. If it describes a particular workflow, technique, prompt instruction, or implementation detail, it likely belongs elsewhere.

### Standard Structure

Unless otherwise noted, every Design Principle contains: Status, Origin, Confidence, Principle, Rationale, Operational Test, Cross References.

Cross References should identify the relevant Design Constitution principles or safeguards, supporting Evidence Base findings (including the required "source recovery pending" notation where applicable), and Research Change Log entries only when those citations are directly earned by the principle being presented.

The standard of this document is not merely that citations exist. **The standard is that every citation is earned rather than attached.** That principle applies equally to constitutional references, research findings, experimental records, Master Reference case studies, and future governance artifacts.

### Organization of the Document

**Part I — Recognition.** Defines how recognition is created. Governs the construction of evidence, the distinct contribution of clues, and the kind of solving experience the completed puzzle should produce.

**Part II — The Learner Experience.** Defines how recognition is protected. Governs the learner's cognitive difficulty budget, the appropriate use of instructional support, and the treatment of uncertainty, mistakes, and revealed answers as legitimate parts of learning.

**Part III — Author Judgment.** Defines how authors and reviewers exercise professional judgment. Establishes enduring standards for evaluating coherence, resolving competing design choices, and selecting human experiences that fulfill the Design Constitution without prescribing particular authoring methods.

### Part I — Recognition

Part I defines how a puzzle creates recognition. It governs the nature of the clues, the distinct contribution of each clue, and the kind of solving experience the completed board should produce.

#### DP-001 — Recognition Comes from Evidence, Not Labels

**Status:** Working
**Origin:** Design Constitution Principle 1 — Recognition Over Categorization · Design Constitution Principle 3 — Trust the Player · Evidence Base Findings #2 and #6 · RCL-003
**Confidence:** High

**Principle**

A puzzle should lead the player toward recognition by presenting meaningful evidence rather than naming or categorizing the hidden experience.

Each clue should function like something noticed within a believable human Moment. Individually, the clues remain incomplete. Together, they allow the player's memory, imagination, and understanding of human life to reconstruct what is happening.

Authors should resist telling the player where they are, what category the clues represent, or what conclusion to draw. They should provide distinct evidence strong enough for recognition to become the player's own accomplishment.

**Rationale**

Labels identify. Evidence allows reconstruction.

If clues merely name the subject, state the answer, or function as category members, the player performs classification rather than rebuilding a lived experience.

When clues reveal different pieces of the same human Moment, prior knowledge and ordinary-life understanding can connect those fragments into a coherent scene.

The intended realization is "Oh—I know what is happening," not "Those words belong to the same category."

**Operational Test**

For every clue, ask: *Does this clue provide evidence from inside the experience, or does it merely identify, summarize, or categorize the answer?*

Then test: Could a person plausibly notice, hear, say, feel, or experience this within the Moment? Does the clue allow inference rather than state the conclusion? Does it contribute to reconstructing a scene rather than naming a topic? Would replacing it with a category label substantially reduce the puzzle's experiential quality?

If the clue tells the player what the Moment is instead of helping the player recognize it, revise it.

#### DP-002 — Every Clue Should Contribute Different Evidence

**Status:** Working
**Origin:** Design Constitution Principle 4 — Every Clue Earns Its Place · Design Constitution Clue Contribution Test · Evidence Base Findings #4, #5, and #6 · RCL-005
**Confidence:** High

**Principle**

The four clues within a Moment should cooperate by contributing different evidence toward the same recognizable human experience.

Each clue should reveal a distinct aspect of the Moment so that the clues collectively create a richer and more recognizable mental picture than any clue could create alone.

A strong Moment is built from complementary evidence, not repeated evidence.

**Rationale**

Four clues that communicate essentially the same idea do not provide four meaningful pieces of evidence.

Repeated evidence can trap the Moment inside one narrow semantic neighborhood while leaving other recognizable features of the experience unexplored.

Recognition becomes stronger when clues illuminate different facets of the same Moment. The clues should contribute distinct conceptual anchors rather than repeatedly expressing the same topic, mood, or meaning.

The goal is not maximum similarity among clues. The goal is maximum cooperation.

**Operational Test**

For every clue, ask: *If this clue were removed, what unique evidence would disappear?*

Then test: Does the clue reveal something the other three do not? Does it strengthen recognition rather than merely repeat the mood, topic, or meaning of another clue? Does it give the player an additional reason to recognize the intended Moment? Are the four clues cooperating across distinct conceptual anchors rather than clustering inside one vocabulary family? Would removing the clue leave a noticeable gap in the reconstructed experience?

If little or no meaningful evidence disappears when a clue is removed, that clue has not yet earned its place.

#### DP-003 — The Puzzle Should Reward Recognition, Not Elimination

**Status:** Working
**Origin:** Design Constitution Principle 1 — Recognition Over Categorization · Design Constitution Principle 5 — Protect the Learning Experience · Puzzle Constitution — Ambiguity Standard and Alternate-Solution Audit · Evidence Base Findings #2 and #11 · RCL-003
**Confidence:** High

**Principle**

Players should succeed because the intended Moment becomes increasingly recognizable—not merely because incorrect combinations have been exhausted or only one group remains.

As evidence accumulates, the player's confidence should grow through understanding.

Elimination may assist solving, as it does in many grouping puzzles, but it should not be the primary source of resolution. The player should feel that the clues converged into a recognizable human experience.

**Rationale**

Logical elimination can be satisfying, but it is not the central learning experience this game is designed to create.

The educational value lies in the player connecting meaningful fragments and arriving at "I recognize this," rather than "These must go together because nothing else is left."

If recognition occurs only after the board has been mechanically narrowed, the puzzle may be technically solvable without fulfilling its purpose.

This principle therefore evaluates the experience produced by the assembled board, not merely the quality of individual clues.

**Operational Test**

During independent playtesting, ask: *What actually caused the player to solve this Moment—recognition or exhaustion of alternatives?*

Then test: Did the player identify a coherent human experience before only four Tiles remained? Did confidence increase as meaningful evidence accumulated? Could the Moment be recognized without relying primarily on previously solved groups? After the reveal, did the grouping feel understood rather than merely confirmed? Would stronger or more distinctive evidence reduce dependence on guessing and elimination?

If players repeatedly arrive at the intended group only because no alternatives remain, revise the Moment or board structure.

**Cross References**
Design Constitution — Principle 1, Principle 5, Recognition Test, Ambiguity Audit, Final Author Check. Puzzle Constitution — Ambiguity Standard, Alternate-Solution Audit. Evidence Base — Finding #2, Finding #11 (both source recovery pending). Research Change Log — RCL-003.

### Part II — The Learner Experience

Part II governs the learner's experience while interacting with the puzzle. If Part I explains how recognition is created, Part II explains how recognition is protected. These principles govern the conditions under which recognition can occur fairly, confidently, and without unnecessary barriers. Together they ensure that challenge serves learning rather than replacing it.

The principles in this section address three distinct responsibilities: protecting the learner's cognitive difficulty budget during solving; providing support that preserves discovery rather than replacing it; and ensuring that uncertainty, mistakes, and revealed answers remain part of the learning experience rather than becoming experiences of failure. Although these principles work together, each governs a different stage of the learner's interaction with the puzzle and therefore addresses a different potential failure mode.

#### DP-004 — Protect the Learner's Cognitive Difficulty Budget

**Status:** Working
**Origin:** Design Constitution Principle 5 — Protect the Learning Experience · Puzzle Constitution — Difficulty · Evidence Base Findings #7 and #11 · RCL-006
**Confidence:** High

**Principle**

Every puzzle asks the learner to solve two problems at once: understand enough Spanish to work with the clues; reconstruct the lived experience those clues represent. Authors should ensure that these two demands support rather than compete with one another.

Difficulty should arise primarily from meaningful inference—not from obscure vocabulary, unnecessary ambiguity, visual clutter, or several simultaneous sources of confusion.

**Rationale**

Beginners have limited cognitive resources available while solving in another language.

When every clue is difficult for a different reason, the learner's attention shifts away from recognition and toward managing confusion.

A well-designed puzzle spends the learner's effort on the intended challenge: using meaningful evidence to recognize a coherent human experience.

This principle governs the puzzle's cognitive demands. It does not, by itself, address the Constitution's additional commitments to non-shaming language, supported curiosity, or revealed answers as part of learning. Those responsibilities are addressed separately in DP-005 and DP-009.

**Operational Test**

For each Moment and for the puzzle as a whole, ask: *Where is the difficulty coming from?* Identify whether the demand comes from Spanish decoding, clue ambiguity, Moment overlap, visual load, inference, or another source. If several unrelated sources of difficulty are stacked together, reduce at least one.

The learner should be challenged by meaningful inference rather than forced to manage avoidable confusion.

**Cross References**
Design Constitution — Principle 5, Ambiguity Audit, Final Author Check. Puzzle Constitution — Difficulty, Ambiguity Standard, Ambiguity ↔ Mistake Budget. Evidence Base — Finding #7, Finding #11 (both source recovery pending). Research Change Log — RCL-006.

#### DP-005 — Support Should Preserve Discovery

**Status:** Working
**Origin:** Design Constitution Principle 3 — Trust the Player · Design Constitution Principle 5 — Protect the Learning Experience · Foundation — Design Promise · Puzzle Constitution — English Support · Authoring Prompt v0.3a — Core Philosophy and Final Author Check
**Confidence:** High

**Principle**

Support should help the player remain inside the act of recognition without replacing the player's judgment, imagination, or emotional interpretation.

Translations, hints, organization, reveals, and other scaffolds should provide enough access for the learner to continue thinking while preserving the satisfaction of completing the experience themselves.

Authors should provide meaningful evidence without over-explaining it. They should not narrate the emotion of the Moment, tell the player what the experience means, or complete the human interpretation on the player's behalf.

The learner should feel accompanied rather than managed or rescued.

**Rationale**

Too little support can turn curiosity into frustration. Too much support can remove the discovery that gives the puzzle its meaning.

Trusting the player means believing that meaningful evidence is enough when the puzzle is well designed. The author supplies the fragments; the player connects them through memory, imagination, and understanding of human life.

This also protects the game from manufactured emotion. The puzzle may present a goodbye, a gesture, a sound, or a physical detail, but it should not instruct the player to feel sadness, tenderness, nostalgia, or any other response.

Support should preserve access while leaving recognition and interpretation with the player.

This principle governs the learner's experience during the act of solving. The distinct responsibilities surrounding mistakes, unsuccessful completion, and answer reveals are addressed separately in DP-009.

**Operational Test**

Ask: *Does this support help the player continue thinking, or does it think and feel for the player?*

Then test: Does it clarify language without revealing the grouping? Does it provide access without naming the hidden Moment? Does it preserve uncertainty long enough for recognition to occur? Does it avoid narrating emotion or explaining what the scene is supposed to mean? After using the support, does the player still complete an important part of the experience themselves?

If the support supplies the inference, interpretation, or emotional conclusion, reduce it.

Design Constitution — Principle 3, Principle 5, Clue Contribution Test, Final Author Check. Foundation — Design Promise, Emotional Architecture. Puzzle Constitution — English Support. Evidence Base — Finding #2, Finding #7, Finding #12 (all source recovery pending). Research Change Log — RCL-003, RCL-006.

#### DP-009 — Treat Uncertainty and Revelation as Part of Learning

**Status:** Working
**Origin:** Design Constitution Principle 5 — Protect the Learning Experience · Puzzle Constitution — Mistakes and Completion · Foundation — Emotional Architecture · Evidence Base Findings #3 and #11
**Confidence:** High regarding the constitutional obligation; Medium regarding the eventual implementation, which remains Working.

**Principle**

A player's uncertainty, mistakes, and need to see revealed answers must be treated as legitimate parts of learning rather than evidence of failure.

The experience should preserve dignity and curiosity whether the player solves the puzzle independently, solves only part of it, reaches the mistake limit, or completes the experience through revealed answers.

Authors and designers should not use shame, ridicule, disappointment, exaggerated failure language, or punitive framing to create stakes.

The reveal should help the player see the intended relationships and complete the act of recognition, even when they did not discover every relationship alone.

**Rationale**

A language learner may understand more after an answer is revealed than they could demonstrate while solving.

A revealed answer can still create a genuine moment of recognition even when it follows an unsuccessful attempt at recall. Learning therefore continues through the reveal rather than ending when independent solving ends.

Treating the reveal as failure mistakes performance for learning and undermines the confidence the game exists to build.

Uncertainty can signal active thinking. A mistake can narrow possibilities. A revealed answer can make previously disconnected clues suddenly meaningful.

This does not require artificial praise or the removal of challenge. It requires neutral, respectful framing that allows the learner to remain curious and recognize what the puzzle was showing them.

**Operational Test**

Review every error, limit, completion, and reveal state and ask: *Does this state help the player understand more, or merely inform them that they failed?*

Then test: Is the language neutral, respectful, and free of shame? Does the experience distinguish an incorrect submission from a deficient learner? When answers are revealed, can the player still see why the clues belong together? Does the reveal complete or strengthen recognition rather than simply expose the solution? Is curiosity still invited after independent solving ends? Does the experience avoid both punishment and hollow congratulation?

If the state closes down curiosity, frames revelation as defeat, or makes the learner feel judged rather than informed, revise it.

**Cross References**
Design Constitution — Principle 5, Final Author Check. Foundation — Emotional Architecture, Design Promise. Puzzle Constitution — Mistakes and Completion, Ambiguity ↔ Mistake Budget. Evidence Base — Finding #3, Finding #11 (both source recovery pending).

### Part III — Author Judgment

If Part I defines what creates recognition, and Part II defines how the learner's experience is protected, Part III defines how authors and reviewers exercise professional judgment when making design decisions. These principles do not prescribe a particular authoring workflow. Instead, they establish enduring standards for evaluating competing design choices while allowing specific prompts, methods, and production techniques to evolve over time.

#### DP-006 — Design from Coherence

**Status:** Working
**Origin:** Design Constitution Purpose · Design Constitution Principle 1 — Recognition Over Categorization · Puzzle Constitution — The Moment and The World · World Naturalness Test and founding "Everyday Errands" case study · Evidence Base Findings #1, #2, and #5
**Confidence:** High that coherence is an enduring principle; Medium regarding which authoring methods will most reliably produce it.

**Principle**

The parts of a puzzle should arise from a genuinely coherent human structure rather than being assembled first and explained afterward.

Coherence should be discovered, not retrofitted.

Specific authoring methods may evolve over time, but the requirement that a puzzle's internal structure be genuinely coherent should remain stable.

**Rationale**

A puzzle may appear internally consistent because an author can invent a convincing explanation after its parts have already been chosen. That kind of coherence is persuasive only from the author's perspective.

Design coherence should instead exist independently of the explanation offered after the fact. The relationships among the World, Moments, and clues should be strong enough that they emerge naturally from the underlying human experience rather than depending on author justification.

This principle intentionally governs the quality of the finished structure rather than prescribing the workflow used to produce it.

Future research may recommend different authoring sequences or methods. Whatever those methods become, they should produce genuine coherence rather than reconstructing it after design decisions have already been made.

**Operational Test**

Ask: *Does this puzzle possess coherence that a player could naturally discover, or coherence that only the author can explain?*

Then test: Do the relationships among the World, Moments, and clues arise naturally from the underlying human experience? Could the same structure withstand independent scrutiny without additional author explanation? Has coherence been demonstrated or merely asserted? Would removing the author's explanation weaken the perceived relationships among the puzzle's parts? Is the judgment being based on the quality of the completed structure rather than the particular workflow used to produce it?

If the coherence depends primarily on retrospective explanation, revise the design.

#### DP-007 — Recognition Is the Tie-Breaker

**Status:** Working
**Origin:** Design Constitution Purpose · Design Constitution Principle 1 — Recognition Over Categorization · Design Constitution Principle 2 — Recognition Before Spectacle · Design Constitution Principle 4 — Every Clue Earns Its Place · Foundation — Product Vision and Product Purpose · Authoring Prompt v0.3a — Author Priorities · Evidence Base Findings #2, #6, and #11 · RCL-003
**Confidence:** High

**Principle**

When two design choices are otherwise acceptable, prefer the choice that more strongly supports the player's reconstruction of a recognizable human experience while preserving meaningful discovery.

Recognition is the project's primary design criterion.

Vocabulary usefulness, beginner accessibility, visual simplicity, puzzle elegance, emotional resonance, and other design considerations remain important. When those considerations compete, authors and reviewers should evaluate them according to how well each option serves recognition without violating the learner protections established elsewhere in the Design Principles.

Recognition is therefore not merely one desirable quality among many. It is the governing tie-breaker used to resolve legitimate design trade-offs.

**Rationale**

Designing a puzzle requires choices among options that may each be defensible.

One clue may be easier while another creates a more vivid and recognizable scene. One World may be more elegant while another more naturally reflects how people organize experience. One phrase may be shorter while another provides more distinctive human evidence.

Without an explicit decision hierarchy, those choices can be resolved inconsistently through personal taste, convenience, novelty, or whichever concern feels most urgent in the moment.

This principle establishes a stable hierarchy without allowing recognition to override the Constitution's other protections.

This tie-breaker applies only among options that have already satisfied DP-006's coherence requirement, DP-008's shared-humanity test, and the other applicable constitutional protections.

A choice that increases immediate recognizability by creating ambiguity, unnecessary difficulty, spectacle dependence, over-explanation, shame, or reduced learner access is not a valid application of the tie-breaker. Recognition must be strengthened within the boundaries established by the full design system.

**Operational Test**

When choosing between two otherwise acceptable alternatives, ask: *Which option more strongly helps the player reconstruct a believable human experience while preserving discovery and protecting the learning experience?*

Then test: Which option offers more meaningful and distinctive human evidence? Which option makes the intended Moment or World more naturally recognizable? Does either option rely more heavily on semantic categorization, novelty, spectacle, or author explanation? Does the stronger recognition come at the cost of ambiguity, cognitive overload, over-explanation, reduced accessibility, or learner dignity? Is the decision being driven by the project's purpose or merely by personal preference?

Use recognition to resolve the trade-off only after confirming that both options satisfy the Constitution and the other applicable Design Principles.

#### DP-008 — Design for Shared Humanity, Not Spectacle

**Status:** Working
**Origin:** Design Constitution Principle 2 — Recognition Before Spectacle · Design Constitution Recognition Test · Foundation — Product Hypothesis · Evidence Base Findings #2 and #12 · RCL-003
**Confidence:** High

**Principle**

Choose experiences whose recognizability comes from plausible patterns of human life rather than from novelty, fame, cultural references, dramatic scale, or spectacle.

The experience may be ordinary or significant.

A routine morning, a retirement celebration, a proposal, an airport goodbye, or meeting a new grandchild may all belong in the game if the clues reveal the shared human experience within them.

The author's task is not to avoid milestones or emotionally important events. It is to ensure that the puzzle works because players can recognize the humanity—not merely because they recognize the event, reference, or spectacle.

**Rationale**

Spectacle can attract attention without creating meaningful recognition.

Celebrity, fiction, historical events, memes, and culturally specific references may allow players to identify what is being referenced, but that is different from reconstructing a believable human experience through language.

Conversely, significant life events should not be excluded merely because they are unusual or memorable. Milestones often contain deeply recognizable patterns: waiting, preparing, touching, hesitating, gathering, leaving, celebrating, or saying goodbye.

The governing distinction is therefore not ordinary versus extraordinary. It is shared humanity versus spectacle-dependent recognition.

**Operational Test**

Ask: *If the novelty, fame, cultural reference, or dramatic scale were removed, would the clues still reveal a recognizable pattern of human life?*

Also ask: Is the player recognizing human behavior or merely identifying a known event? Could someone who lacks the specific cultural or media reference still reconstruct the experience? Does the Moment remain believable through concrete human evidence? Is its emotional or memorable quality earned by the scene rather than announced by the premise?

If the puzzle becomes interesting or solvable only because the subject is famous, unusual, fictional, historical, or culturally pre-known, revise it.

**Cross References**
Design Constitution — Principle 2, Recognition Test, Principle 3. Foundation — Product Hypothesis, Content Philosophy. Evidence Base — Finding #2, Finding #12 (both source recovery pending). Research Change Log — RCL-003.

**Part III Summary:** Together, these principles ensure that coherence is genuine rather than retrofitted; that recognition governs legitimate design trade-offs without overriding constitutional protections; and that the project chooses experiences whose power comes from shared humanity rather than spectacle.
