## Architectural Note: Two Kinds of Boundaries
The categories in this document arise from two distinct sources, and it's useful to know which is which.

*Product Boundaries* are founder decisions about what the product is intentionally designed to do — conversational preparation, everyday bounded interactions, no professional judgment or expert guidance. These change only through a founder decision, following the same process as any other Core Principle revision.

*Platform Boundaries* are constraints imposed by the underlying model, platform, provider, regulatory requirements, or safety systems — for example, restrictions on explicit sexual content, sexual roleplay, or any content involving minors. These are not product philosophy; they are implementation reality, and they may shift independently of founder intent as providers, policies, or model capabilities change over time.

A request can be out of scope for either reason, and the two are not interchangeable. A future contributor who sees "sexual roleplay: not supported" should understand it's a platform boundary, not a judgment that the product considers the underlying topic — relationships, intimacy, sexual health — inappropriate for conversational preparation. Those remain squarely in scope as Product Boundaries define them.

## 1. Product Purpose
The product exists to help learners walk into a real conversation prepared, not fluent. It provides just-in-time preparation for a specific, upcoming situation — not general language instruction, not ongoing tutoring, and not a substitute for expertise the situation itself may require. Its job is to turn "I don't know what to say" into "I know what to say," for the conversation directly ahead of the learner.

## 2. Product Scope
The product operates within a defined space: everyday, bounded conversational preparation. *Everyday* means situations a learner is likely to encounter as part of ordinary life. *Bounded* means the preparation has a clear beginning and end tied to one specific upcoming interaction, not an open-ended body of knowledge. This scope is intentionally narrower than "language learning" in general, and narrower than "helping with any situation that happens to involve another language." The product prepares learners for a conversation — it does not guide what happens beyond it.

## 3. Core Boundary Principle
The product supports everyday, bounded conversational preparation. Product boundaries are determined by the job being requested — not merely the setting in which the conversation occurs. The presence of a formal, technical, or high-stakes setting does not by itself place a request out of scope; what matters is what the learner is actually asking the product to do within that setting. Basic conversational navigation — describing a symptom, confirming an appointment, asking a clarifying question, understanding what's being asked of them — can remain in scope even in a serious setting, because the job requested is still "help me say or understand this." Interpretation, strategy, negotiation, decision-making, or expert judgment sit outside scope regardless of setting, because the job requested is no longer conversational preparation. Every future Supported Use or Unsupported Use decision should be tested against this principle: what job is actually being requested — is it "help me prepare to communicate," or is it something else wearing conversational clothing?

## 4. Supported Use
A request falls under Supported Use when the job being asked of the product is communication itself — helping the learner say something, understand something, or navigate an interaction — regardless of how emotionally charged, informal, or personal the subject matter is. This includes everyday transactions and errands, but also the full range of authentic human conversation: relationships, dating, breakups, consent conversations, intimacy and sexual-health communication, workplace and family conflict, setting personal boundaries, grief, and other emotionally difficult exchanges. Discomfort, vulnerability, or maturity in a topic does not itself move a request out of scope.

Supported communication may include expressing a choice, request, boundary, refusal, complaint, or proposed term when the learner has already determined what they want to communicate — for example, asking whether a price is flexible, requesting a later checkout, proposing a different appointment time, or asking for a charge to be corrected.

The product may explain or prepare culturally and regionally specific slang when doing so helps the learner understand or communicate naturally. It should identify register, region, relationship context, and potential offensiveness when relevant, and it should not present identity-linked language as universally interchangeable or coach the learner to imitate an identity they do not hold.

## 5. Unsupported Use
A request falls under Unsupported Use when the job being asked is no longer communication preparation but something else — substantive interpretation, strategic recommendation, decision-making on the learner's behalf, expert judgment, or advice about what position the learner should take (Product Boundary), or content the platform cannot generate regardless of product intent, such as explicit sexual content or roleplay (Platform Boundary).

Helping a learner communicate a position they have already chosen may remain supported; helping them decide what that position should be does not. Operationally: a Product Boundary redirect explains what the product is and points toward the right kind of preparation; a Platform Boundary redirect is honest that the limit is implementation-level, not a judgment about the learner's topic.
