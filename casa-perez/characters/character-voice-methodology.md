## The Three Lenses (Solara's discovery)

Not different personalities. Different lenses on the same person.

1. **Everyday Sofía (YouTube).** Talking to us. Jokes about herself, her situation, her life. Present-tense, self-referential.
2. **Agrupa Sofía (Reflection).** Not talking about herself — talking about the world she just shared with the player. Nearly invisible as a narrator. She simply notices.
3. **Casa Pérez Sofía (family chat).** Participating in conversation with others. A third register again.

Agrupa's own working rule (see `../projects/agrupa/sofia-observations-bank.md`) is that everything collected and used for Reflection should come from Lens 2 — noticing the world, not narrating herself.

## Character Principle — Dialogue Is Downstream of Attention

*Before we can consistently write what a character would say, we have to understand what they consistently notice.*

Vocabulary, cadence, humor, and sentence structure all change depending on context. Attention is more stable. Attention transfers across contexts. **Therefore, a character's observational habits are a more fundamental layer of character than their dialogue style.** This is not an Agrupa-specific finding — it's a claim about how to build a believable fictional person, and it should govern character work generally.

## Foundational Principle — What Sofía Notices

Sofía's attention naturally gravitates toward small moments that reveal something about ordinary people. She is less interested in systems, objects, coincidence, or irony than she is in the quiet patterns of everyday human life.

Every diagnostic below exists to protect this principle. **They are diagnostics, not the philosophy itself** — the goal is for future authors to internalize how she sees the world, not to memorize a checklist. If a diagnostic ever seems to justify something that doesn't reveal a person, the diagnostic is being misapplied; the Foundational Principle governs the diagnostics, not the reverse.

## Human Recognition Diagnostic (the test)

**Operational test:** is a person's psychology, behavior, or social dynamic the actual *cause* of the pattern being described — or does a person just happen to be the one noticing an independently-existing mechanical or systemic fact? The last slice sits for twenty minutes *because* of politeness — the social dynamic is the mechanism, not incidental to it. A receipt prints *because* the register is built to print one — a person not wanting it is incidental to why it exists.

**Three buckets:**
- **Bucket A — Human Behavior ✅.** People doing recognizably human things. Saving a seat, pretending a coffee order is simple, "just a light trim," the WiFi password question.
- **Bucket B — Shared Physical/Sensory Experience ✅ (smaller bucket).** Not about behavior — about something nearly everyone has physically felt. "Warm towels deserve more credit." No social behavior occurs, but the *feeling itself* is the mechanism.
- **Bucket C — Situational Mechanics ❌.** Describes circumstances, not humans. Receipts, clocks, puzzle pieces, umbrellas, parking spots, radio signal. True regardless of who's there.

**Working observation, untested against a larger batch, flag as a hypothesis rather than a confirmed rule:** the *same underlying phenomenon* can cross buckets depending on framing, not just subject matter. "The waiting room clock runs slower than every other clock" (Bucket C — stated as an external fact about the clock) versus "Everyone swears the waiting room clock is broken" (Bucket A — stated as a human belief/complaint; the mechanism is boredom warping time perception, which is genuinely human). Same with "the last piece is never where you left it" (Bucket C, mysterious external fact) versus "I will absolutely forget where I put that piece" (Bucket A, admission of human behavior). **If this holds up across more examples, it suggests some Bucket C rejects aren't dead ends — they may be rescuable by reframing as a human belief or admission rather than an objective claim about the object.**
